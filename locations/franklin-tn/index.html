Why fire damage in Franklin is different

Franklin mixes two very different housing stocks. Around Franklin Square and the historic downtown, many homes date to the 1800s and early 1900s. They often have plaster walls, heart-pine floors, tall ceilings, original trim and chimneys that were built long before modern fire codes. Some of these houses have had wiring updated in stages, so old and new circuits can share the same walls.

The other half of Franklin is newer. Subdivisions built since the 1990s, including large homes in Westhaven and neighborhoods near Cool Springs, often have open floor plans, two-story family rooms, gas fireplaces and finished bonus rooms over garages. Open layouts let smoke move quickly through the whole house, and tall ceilings collect soot that is hard to reach without scaffolding.

Franklin's population reached 83,454 in the 2020 census, making it one of the fastest-growing cities in Middle Tennessee. Growth brings a lot of new construction. But it also means many homes have similar finishes, which can help when matching cabinets and flooring after a fire.

Tips for Franklin homeowners

  • Historic homes: Photograph original trim, mantels and hardware before cleanup begins, so pieces can be cleaned, saved or matched later.
  • Fireplace owners: Have chimneys inspected before winter and keep anything that can burn at least three feet from heating equipment, as NFPA advises.
  • Bonus rooms over garages: Store fuel, paint and solvents in approved containers away from water heaters and dryers.
  • HOA neighborhoods: Check whether your HOA must approve exterior repairs, siding colors or roofing before the rebuild starts.
  • Insurance: Larger Franklin homes can have high replacement costs. So review your dwelling limit and ask your agent about extended replacement cost coverage.

Permits and historic considerations in Franklin

Franklin runs its own building department, separate from Metro Nashville Codes, so fire repair permits go through the City of Franklin. Williamson County is not on Tennessee's home improvement license list. But the state contractor license still applies to any project of $25,000 or more, which covers most fire rebuilds. Properties inside Franklin's historic districts may need design review before exterior materials, windows or rooflines change. Ask the provider to confirm review requirements before ordering replacement windows or siding.

Franklin's history is part of why this matters. The 1864 Battle of Franklin left several preserved houses in the area, including Carnton, and the city has worked hard to protect its historic streetscape. Restoration in these neighborhoods often means cleaning and saving original materials rather than replacing them.

Fire damage restoration in Franklin questions

Do providers cover all of Franklin?

Yes. Providers in this network serve all Franklin zip codes, including 37064, 37067 and 37069, from historic downtown and Fieldstone Farms to Westhaven, Cool Springs and the Mallory Lane corridor.

Who issues permits for fire repairs in Franklin?

The City of Franklin handles building permits inside city limits. Homes in unincorporated Williamson County work through the county. The provider can tell you which office applies to your address.

Can a historic Franklin home be restored after a fire?

Often, yes. Plaster, heart-pine floors and original trim can frequently be cleaned, repaired or matched. Design review may apply in historic districts, so plan time for approvals.

How do I get smoke smell out of a large two-story home?

Smoke spreads quickly through open layouts and tall ceilings, so every level and the HVAC system usually need cleaning before deodorizing. Source removal comes first, then treatments such as thermal fogging or hydroxyl generators.

Should I move out during restoration?

If smoke reached living areas or the HVAC system, or if rooms are being torn out, staying elsewhere is usually safer. Many homeowners policies include additional living expenses coverage for this.
